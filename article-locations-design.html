I. Introduction: Environments as Narrative Engines

In Love and Deepspace, locations are not mere backdrops. They are active participants in storytelling — each environment carries its own emotional weight, its own visual language, its own secrets. The game's designers have created a world where you can understand a character's internal state simply by looking at where they stand, how light falls on their face, and what surrounds them.

Environmental Storytelling Principles in Deepspace:
  • Color temperature as emotional shorthand: Warm light = intimacy/danger, cool light = safety/distance
  • Architectural scale as power dynamic: Who stands above, who stands below
  • Environmental clutter as character window: What characters keep in their spaces reveals who they are
  • Lighting direction as moral framing: Harsh shadows for moral ambiguity, soft light for vulnerability

This guide will walk through every major location in Love and Deepspace, analyzing the design choices that make each space unforgettable. From the gleaming towers of Tianxing City to the rain-soaked alleys of N109 Zone, from the sterile corridors of Gaia Research Center to the intimate warmth of MC's room — each place tells a story that words alone cannot convey.

II. Tianxing City — The Gilded Cage

Tianxing City is the first major location players encounter after the prologue. It is presented as a utopia — a "miracle of the core energy era" — but its visual language betrays a darker truth. Every gleaming surface, every holographic advertisement, every perfectly maintained street is a mask hiding something rotten.

Color Palette: Cool Blues, Sterile Whites, Occasional Gold

The dominant colors of Tianxing are cool — blues, cyans, whites. These colors psychologically signal distance, professionalism, and control. Unlike the warm reds of N109, Tianxing does not invite intimacy. It impresses, but it does not embrace.

The gold accents (on the Fleet headquarters, on official buildings) are the only warm elements — but gold is the color of value, not warmth. It says "you are looking at something expensive," not "you are welcome here."

2.1 Urban Planning as Ideology

Tianxing City's layout follows a radial-concentric pattern — the Farspace Fleet headquarters at the center, with residential and commercial zones radiating outward. This is not accidental. Radial city planning is historically associated with authoritarian power structures (think Haussmann's Paris, or Albert Speer's plans for Berlin). The message is clear: everything in Tianxing exists in relation to the Fleet. There is no "outside."

Urban Design Details That Tell a Story:
  • The absence of street-level commerce: All shops are in elevated plazas or underground. The street level is for transit, not human interaction.
  • Overabundance of surveillance cameras: Visible on every corner. The state is always watching.
  • Holographic advertisements as information control: They don't sell products — they sell ideology. "Core Energy: Power for a Better Tomorrow."
  • The "gates" between districts: Checkpoints that require ID. Movement is monitored, controlled, restricted.

2.2 The Farspace Fleet Headquarters

The Fleet headquarters is the architectural heart of Tianxing — a towering structure of glass, steel, and blue energy conduits. Its design language is brutalist-modernist: massive, imposing, unadorned. It does not welcome you; it overwhelms you.

Image descriptions: Left — exterior shot of the monolithic Fleet HQ at night, blue energy pulsing up its sides. Right — interior corridor, impossibly long, harsh fluorescent lighting, no windows.

The Interior Experience:

The Fleet HQ interior is designed to disorient. Endless corridors, identical doors, no natural light. Ceilings are high (creating insignificance), floors are polished to a mirror shine (creating disorientation). The few windows face inward toward central atriums, never outward. You cannot see the sky from inside the Fleet HQ. This is a building that wants you to forget there is a world outside its walls.

Xia Yizhou's office is the only space with a window — and it faces the city, not the sky. He can watch over Tianxing, but he cannot see the stars. A deliberate visual metaphor for his position: power, but no freedom.

2.3 The Hospital & Zayne's Domain

The hospital where Zayne works is a study in sterile aesthetic as emotional armor. Every surface is white or pale gray. Lighting is fluorescent — harsh, flattening, revealing. There are no decorative elements, no personal touches in public areas.

Figure 3: The hospital — cold, clinical, but Zayne's presence warms it slightly.

Image description: Hospital corridor, white walls, fluorescent lights, Zayne standing in his white coat, the only warm element in a cold composition.

Zayne's Office — The Only Warm Space:

Zayne's personal office is different. Here, there are books. A kettle for tea. A small plant on the windowsill. These small personal touches transform the space from clinical to intimate. The contrast between the sterile hospital and Zayne's office tells you everything about his character: he performs professionalism in public, but in private, he allows himself small comforts. The only times MC sees his office are during moments of vulnerability — when he is being Zayne, not "Dr. Zayne."

III. N109 Zone — The Beautiful Abyss

If Tianxing City is a gilded cage, N109 Zone is a beautiful abyss. It is everything Tianxing is not: warm-colored, chaotic, dangerous, and alive. The visual language of N109 is cyberpunk distilled — but with a unique melancholic undertone that separates it from genre clichés.

Color Palette: Deep Reds, Neon Purples, Warm Ambers

Unlike Tianxing's cool blues, N109 is warm — but warmth here signals danger, not comfort. Red lights in cyberpunk aesthetics traditionally indicate danger, vice, and passion. The purple neon suggests mystery and transformation. The amber glow of streetlights creates pools of false safety in darkness.

The sky in N109 is never visible — only the undersides of upper levels, pipes, and cables. You cannot see the stars here either, but for different reasons than Tianxing. In Tianxing, the sky is hidden by architecture designed to control. In N109, the sky is hidden by neglect — no one has bothered to build a ceiling you can see through.

3.1 Brutalist Architecture & Cyberpunk Aesthetics

N109 Zone is built from the bones of an older city — brutalist concrete structures now overlaid with neon, holograms, and makeshift additions. The juxtaposition is key: grandiose decay. These buildings were once important. Now they are slums, but the scale remains. The ceilings are still high, the corridors still wide — but they are filled with debris, market stalls, and people trying to survive.

Architectural Details That Tell a Story:
  • Exposed infrastructure: Pipes, cables, ducts — nothing is hidden. N109 does not pretend to be anything other than what it is.
  • The verticality of poverty: The wealthy live on upper levels (closer to what little light exists). The poor live below, in perpetual twilight.
  • Makeshift additions: Shacks built into old building frames, staircases that lead nowhere, doors that open to walls. The city has been modified so many times that its original plan is illegible.
  • Water everywhere: Leaking pipes, rain, condensation. Water in N109 symbolizes persistence — the city is always leaking, always wet, but never clean.

Image description: First-person perspective of an N109 alley, red and purple neon signs, steam rising from a grate, puddles reflecting light, oppressive verticality.

3.2 Sylus's Throne Room

Sylus's throne room is the most visually striking location in N109. It subverts expectations: you expect a cyberpunk warlord's lair to be dark, cramped, violent. Instead, Sylus's throne room is open, cathedral-like, and eerily peaceful. High ceilings, warm amber lighting, a throne that is almost understated. The room says: I have nothing to prove. I am already king.

Design Elements of the Throne Room:
  • The throne: Metal, angular, but not ornate. It is functional, not decorative.
  • The light source: Behind the throne, creating a halo effect around Sylus when he sits.
  • The emptiness: No guards, no furniture, no clutter. Sylus does not need to surround himself with objects.
  • The window (if you can call it that): A massive screen displaying a false sky — stars that aren't real. Sylus cannot see real stars either, but he has built his own.

Image description: Sylus sitting on his throne, amber backlight creating a halo, the room vast and empty, a false starfield on a massive screen behind him.

The Throne Room as Character Window:

Sylus's throne room tells you everything about him: he is powerful (the scale), but he is also lonely (the emptiness). He has built a false sky to look at because he cannot see the real one. The throne is not comfortable — it is a statement. The room is beautiful but cold, impressive but sad. This is not the lair of a villain; it is the cage of a man who has everything and nothing.

3.3 The Hidden Laboratories

Beneath N109 Zone are EVER's hidden laboratories — the true heart of darkness in Love and Deepspace. These spaces are a complete aesthetic departure from the zone above: sterile white, clinical lighting, containment pods. The contrast is jarring. Above: warm, chaotic, alive. Below: cold, ordered, dead.

Figure 5: The hidden laboratory — where Core Source Disease is weaponized, where humans become monsters.

Image description: Sterile white laboratory, rows of cylindrical containment pods with glowing blue interiors, medical equipment, a sense of horror beneath cleanliness.

The Horror of Cleanliness:

The laboratories are not dirty or chaotic. They are too clean. This is a specific horror aesthetic: evil is not always dark and bloody. Sometimes it is bright, white, and organized. The laboratories look like hospitals — but they are not places of healing. The blue glow from the containment pods is the same blue as Tianxing's core energy conduits, the same blue as the Fleet's technology. The visual connection is intentional: the laboratories, the Fleet, and Tianxing are all part of the same system. EVER's cruelty is not an aberration — it is the logical conclusion of Tianxing's ideology.

IV. Deepspace Tunnel — Threshold of Worlds

The Deepspace Tunnel is the most visually spectacular location in Love and Deepspace — and the most narratively significant. It is the threshold between Earth and the unknown, between safety and danger, between ordinary life and the extraordinary. Every major story beat involves crossing through the tunnel, and its visual design shifts depending on the emotional context.

Figure 6: The Deepspace Tunnel as seen from Earth — a rift in the sky, glowing gold, both beautiful and terrifying.

Image description: Night sky over Linkon City, a massive golden rift tearing through the clouds, energy swirling at its edges, a silhouette of the city below.

4.1 Visual Language of Cosmic Horror

The tunnel's interior is a masterpiece of cosmic horror aesthetics. The colors are otherworldly — golds, purples, deep blues — but the shapes are what create unease. There are no straight lines inside the tunnel. Everything curves, swirls, folds in on itself. The architecture (if it can be called that) is non-Euclidean. Your brain cannot fully parse what it is seeing, and that is the point. The tunnel is alien in the most literal sense.

Design Elements That Create Dread:
  • Scale ambiguity: Are those structures miles away or inches? No frame of reference.
  • Organic curves: The tunnel looks alive, breathing, pulsing with energy.
  • Partial visibility: Light comes from unknown sources, creating shadows that shouldn't exist.
  • The "pull": The tunnel narrows toward a point in the distance — the destination is always just out of reach.

Image description: First-person view inside the tunnel, swirling gold and purple energy, organic curved walls, no visible floor or ceiling, a point of light in the distance.

4.2 The Wanderer Emergence Sequence

The moment Wanderers emerge from the tunnel is a masterclass in visual storytelling. The tunnel tears open — not like a door opening, but like a wound. Golden light bleeds out, and from the light, shadows take form. The Wanderers are silhouettes at first, only resolving into monstrous shapes as they approach. The sequence never shows you too much too quickly. Fear of the unknown is more powerful than fear of the known.

Figure 7: The moment of emergence — light bleeding from a wound in reality, a silhouette taking shape.

Image description: A massive golden rift, a dark humanoid silhouette stepping out of the light, energy trailing from its form, a sense of scale impossible to determine.

The Tunnel as Narrative Pivot:

Every time a character enters the tunnel, they are changed. It is a liminal space — a threshold between states of being. Xavier has crossed it more times than anyone, and it has worn him down. MC crosses it and discovers new aspects of her power. Sylus rules the zone nearest to the tunnel's exit. The tunnel is not just a location; it is a force in the narrative, reshaping everyone who comes near it.

V. Gaia Research Center — Cathedral of Cruelty

Gaia Research Center is the birthplace of the Ether Core project — and the site of the game's most brutal environmental storytelling. Unlike the hidden laboratories beneath N109 (which are clinical and cold), Gaia Center is abandoned. It is a ruin, but a ruin of recent cruelty. The aesthetic is not cyberpunk or futuristic — it is brutalist horror.

Image descriptions: Left — Gaia Center exterior, brutalist concrete, overgrown with vegetation, a sense of nature reclaiming cruelty. Right — interior corridor, overturned equipment, scorch marks, empty containment pods with broken glass.

The Architecture of Guilt:

Gaia Center is built like a bunker — thick concrete walls, narrow corridors, no windows. It was designed to keep secrets in. Now it is a tomb for those secrets. The environmental details are devastating:

  • Children's drawings on the walls of a containment room: Someone tried to make this place less horrible for the children being experimented on.
  • A single chair facing a wall: Time-out? Or something worse?
  • Scorch marks that trace the shape of a small body: A Wanderer transformation occurred here, and it was not controlled.
  • Medical records scattered and water-damaged: The truth is being erased by time and neglect — but not completely.

Figure 8: A detail that breaks the heart — crayon drawings of flowers on a containment room wall. A child tried to make this place less frightening.

Image description: Close-up of a concrete wall, faded crayon drawings of stick figures and flowers, the date "2044" written in childish handwriting.

Gaia Center's Role in the Narrative:

MC and Xia Yizhou were both experimented on here. Walking through Gaia Center is walking through their shared trauma. The environmental storytelling forces the player to feel what they cannot say. The abandoned toys, the broken equipment, the walls that have been kicked and punched — every detail adds to the weight of what happened here. Gaia Center is not just a location; it is a character in the story, one that cannot speak but whose presence is felt in every scene that references it.

VI. The Protagonist's Room — Sanctuary of Memory

MC's room is the most intimate space in the game — and one of the most narratively rich. It is where players start each day, where messages are received, where the bond with characters deepens. The room's design has evolved over updates, but its core elements remain consistent.

Figure 9: MC's room — warm, lived-in, full of memory. A sanctuary in a dangerous world.

Image description: MC's bedroom, warm amber lighting, a bed with soft blankets, a desk with personal items, windows showing the city skyline.

What the Room Reveals About MC:
  • The bookshelf: Mixed genres — romance, adventure, textbooks. She is curious, romantic, and practical.
  • The corkboard: Photos, notes, reminders. She is organized, sentimental, and a little obsessive.
  • The plant on the windowsill: She nurtures things. She wants to see things grow.
  • The empty chair: The room is designed for one, but there is space for another. She is open to connection.
  • The view of the city: She is part of something larger, but her room is a refuge from it.

Figure 10: The corkboard — a map of MC's memories and relationships. Some photos are turned face-down.

Image description: Close-up of a corkboard, pinned photos of characters (some face-down), handwritten notes, a pressed flower, a train ticket.

The Room as Emotional Barometer:

The lighting in MC's room changes depending on the story context. During happy moments, the light is warm and golden. During sad or tense moments, it is cooler, dimmer. After traumatic events, the room feels empty even when nothing has physically changed. The designers use environmental cues to mirror MC's emotional state, creating a feedback loop between player and character.

VII. Rafayel's Studio & The Deep Sea Ruins

Rafayel's spaces are the most emotionally expressive of all the male leads. His studio is chaotic, colorful, alive — a direct contrast to Zayne's sterile hospital or Xavier's sparse observatory. The Deep Sea Ruins, tied to his backstory as the Sea God, are melancholic, beautiful, and haunted.

Rafayel's Studio — Controlled Chaos:

Every surface is covered with art supplies. Canvases lean against walls, some finished, some abandoned mid-stroke. The color palette is warm — pinks, purples, golds. But there are also dark corners, shadows where unfinished work hides. The studio says: I am creative, but I am also haunted by what I cannot finish.

Image description: Rafayel's studio, messy but intentional, a canvas on an easel showing a half-finished seascape, paint on the floor, warm purple lighting.

Figure 11: The Deep Sea Ruins — a lost kingdom beneath the waves, beautiful and sad.

Image description: Underwater ruins, crumbling pillars covered in coral, light filtering down from above, a sense of ancient grandeur now lost.

The Deep Sea Ruins — Memory Made Stone:

The ruins are where Rafayel's past as the Sea God lives. They are beautiful — filtered light, ancient architecture, sea life reclaiming stone — but they are also dead. No one lives here anymore. The civilization is gone. Rafayel visits the ruins in his memories, not in person. They represent what he has lost: a kingdom, a people, an identity. The ruins are melancholy made physical.

VIII. Xavier's Observatory — Starlight & Solitude

Xavier's observatory is the quietest space in Love and Deepspace. It is sparsely furnished, almost ascetic. The only significant features are the telescope, the star charts, and the windows — large, facing the sky. Xavier does not need much. He has waited two hundred years; material possessions have lost their meaning.

Figure 12: The observatory — Xavier's refuge, where he watches the stars he cannot reach.

Image description: A circular room, a large telescope pointing at a window showing the night sky, star charts on the walls, minimal furniture, soft blue-white lighting.

Design as Character Reveal:
  • The telescope: Xavier is always looking outward, always searching. He rarely looks at what is in front of him.
  • The star charts: Annotations in his handwriting. He has been tracking the same stars for centuries.
  • The single chair: He lives alone. He expects to be alone.
  • The empty tea cup: A small ritual, a reminder of humanity.
  • The absence of personal photos: He has outlived everyone he ever loved. He does not keep reminders.

Figure 13: The telescope, the star charts, the annotations — two hundred years of watching.

IX. Conclusion: How Place Becomes Feeling

The locations of Love and Deepspace are not just where the story happens — they are how the story is told. Through architecture, lighting, color, and environmental detail, the designers create emotional experiences that dialogue alone cannot achieve.

The Ultimate Insight:

Every location in Love and Deepspace is a character. They have personalities, moods, and arcs. They change as the story changes — Tianxing becomes colder after the truth about EVER is revealed, N109 becomes more dangerous as Sylus's power shifts, MC's room becomes lonelier after Xia Yizhou's return. The designers understand that place is not background; place is presence. And in a game about connection, presence is everything.

The Places We Carry With Us

The worlds of Love and Deepspace stay with players long after they close the game. That is the mark of exceptional environmental design. We remember how Tianxing's blue lights made us feel small. We remember N109's red glow — dangerous and alive. We remember the golden terror of the Deepspace Tunnel, the heartbreaking crayon drawings on Gaia's walls, the quiet solitude of Xavier's observatory.

These places are not real, but the feelings they create are. And perhaps that is the true magic of Deepspace: it builds worlds so carefully, so intentionally, that we cannot help but believe in them — and in the characters who live there.
