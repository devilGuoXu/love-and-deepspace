Five Things the Love and Deepspace Community Gets Wrong

By Kaelen — July 10, 2026 — 20 min read

I have been sitting on these opinions for a while, watching the same advice get repeated across Reddit threads and Discord channels and YouTube guides, and I have finally reached the point where I need to say something. These are not facts. These are opinions, backed by a year of play, hundreds of hours of testing, and way too many spreadsheets. You are allowed to disagree. In fact, I hope you do. That is how the community gets better.

Myth #1: "F2P Players Should Only Pull on Limited Banners"

This is the most commonly repeated piece of advice for new F2P players, and I think it is only half right. The full version of this advice is "save all your gems for limited banners because standard banner SSRs will come naturally through free pulls." And that second part is true. You do get free standard pulls from events, battle passes, and monthly resets. Over the course of a year, a dedicated F2P player gets roughly 150-200 standard banner pulls without spending a single gem.

But here is what nobody says: those 150-200 pulls do not guarantee you anything. I have seen F2P accounts with 180 standard pulls and zero SSRs to show for it. I have seen accounts with 120 pulls and three SSRs. The variance is enormous. The difference between an F2P account that gets early standard SSRs and one that does not is the difference between having a usable main team in month two and struggling through month six with SR memories.

The standard banner has a 0.6% SSR rate and hard pity at 70. If you never pull on the standard banner with gems, you are looking at roughly 10-12 standard banner SSRs per year. That sounds like a lot until you realize that the standard pool has multiple memories, you cannot target specific ones, and you will get duplicates that convert to a paltry number of shards. You could easily go a year without getting the standard SSR you actually want.

Here is my hot take: if you are a new F2P player and you have no usable SSRs after your first month, you should absolutely spend some gems on the standard banner. Not all of them. Not the majority. But 20-30 pulls on the standard banner to fill out your roster is a reasonable investment that accelerates your early game progress significantly. The "never spend gems on standard" advice was written by launch players who had a year of free pulls building up. It does not apply the same way to someone starting today.

The risk is small. Limited banners rerun. The meta changes. Missing one limited banner does not ruin your account. But being stuck with a team of SR memories for three months while your standard banner pity sits at 50 because you were following advice that did not apply to your situation? That can ruin your motivation to keep playing. And a player who quits is a player who never spends anything.

The optimal strategy for a launch player and the optimal strategy for a new player are not the same. Advice has an expiration date.

Myth #2: "Protocore Sets Matter More Than Stats"

I see this constantly in build advice threads. Someone posts their build, and the first comment is always "you should be running 4pc Lightseeker instead of 2pc/2pc." And sure, set bonuses are important. The 4pc bonuses on most sets provide significant damage increases. But the community has developed an almost superstitious reverence for set bonuses that ignores the actual math.

Here is the reality: a 2pc/2pc setup with perfect substats outperforms a 4pc set with garbage substats. Every single time. I tested this on my Xavier using the training dummy over 50 runs per configuration. My 2pc/2pc setup with CRIT Rate main stat and four useful substat lines averaged 23,400 DPS. My 4pc Lightseeker set with HP% main stat and two useful substat lines averaged 19,800 DPS. The 4pc bonus was worth about 15% damage. The substat quality difference was worth about 18%. The better substats won.

I am not saying set bonuses do not matter. I am saying that the community prioritizes them at the wrong point in the farming cycle. Early and mid-game, when your protocore inventory is thin, running mismatched sets with good stats is completely fine. You should only start chasing specific set bonuses once every piece in your current setup has at least two usable substat lines. Until then, the substats are carrying more of your damage than the set bonus.

The corollary to this myth is the belief that you need to farm a specific domain for months to get the right set. People spend weeks in a single domain, running it hundreds of times, trashing every piece that is not from the right set, even if the wrong-set piece has better stats. That is backwards. Take the good stats wherever you can find them. The set bonus will come when it comes. Forcing it just means you spend more time farming and less time playing.

A Concrete Example My Sylus ran 2pc HP + 2pc ATK for four months because I never got a usable 4pc planetary set. When I finally did, the new set was a 6% DPS increase over the mismatched pieces. Six percent. For four months of suboptimal performance. Was it worth waiting? In hindsight, absolutely not. I should have just leveled the 2pc/2pc and enjoyed the 94% performance for four months instead of stressing about the 6% I was missing.

Myth #3: "You Need Multiple DPS Operatives for Abyss"

Abyss requires two teams. Two teams need two main DPS operatives. This is technically true. But the community has built an entire dogma around this that leads players to spread their resources too thin too early.

Here is what I have learned through trial and error: you need one main DPS, one secondary DPS, and everyone else can be a support. A support in this context does not need high-level memories or good protocores. A support needs to stay alive, apply their buffs or debuffs, and swap out. You can clear Abyss floors 1-9 with one hyper-invested DPS, one moderately invested secondary DPS, and a team of supports running level 50 memories with garbage protocores.

I tested this on my alt account. I took my main DPS Sylus to level 80 with +15 protocores. My secondary DPS Xavier was level 60 with +9 protocores. My three supports were level 50 with +6 protocores and no set bonuses. I cleared floor 9 with this setup. Floor 10 took some retries but I got it. The supports were basically there to activate Stellactrum matching and provide resonance chains. They did not need to do damage. They needed to exist.

The community advice to "build two full teams evenly" is a resource trap. It sounds sensible, but it ignores the reality that resources are scarce and that a 20% investment in a support yields maybe 5% of the return that a 20% investment in your main DPS would. You are better off having one god-tier operative carry two teams than having two mediocre operatives each carry their own team. The damage distribution is not equal in practice. It is heavily weighted toward the on-field DPS.

I see accounts with four operatives at level 70 and no one above 70. I would rather see one operative at level 80, one at 70, and two at 50. That account will clear more content. — A thought I have had while reviewing way too many account help threads

Myth #4: "CRIT Rate Is Always Better Than ATK%"

The substat hierarchy in the community is basically set in stone: CRIT Rate > CRIT DMG > ATK% > Energy Regen > Everything else. And this hierarchy is mostly correct for late-game accounts with high base stats. But it gets applied as universal truth without considering the actual state of the account.

CRIT stats are only valuable if you have enough base ATK to make the crits matter. An operative with 1,200 base ATK and 60% CRIT Rate is doing less damage than an operative with 1,600 base ATK and 30% CRIT Rate, because the crits are multiplying a smaller number. ATK% is the foundation that crit stats build on. If your ATK is low, crit stats are a luxury you cannot afford yet.

I have a spreadsheet where I modeled damage output across different stat distributions. For an operative with base 1,000 ATK (typical for a level 60 memory setup), the optimal substat distribution is approximately 45% ATK substats and 35% CRIT substats. For an operative with base 1,800 ATK (typical for a fully built level 80 operative with good memories), the optimal shifts to approximately 25% ATK and 55% CRIT. The crossover point is around 1,400 base ATK.

What this means in practice: early and mid-game players who follow the CRIT-first advice are sabotaging their damage. They stack CRIT Rate substats thinking they are optimizing, when what they actually need is ATK% to raise their damage floor. The CRIT-heavy build only outperforms once you have enough base stats to support it.

I am not saying CRIT is bad. I am saying the hierarchy is context-dependent, and the community has lost sight of that. If your main DPS has less than 1,400 ATK on their stat screen, you should prioritize ATK% substats over CRIT subs. Check the math yourself. Build a spreadsheet. Do not take my word for it, and do not take the community's word for it either.

The Breakpoints I Use
Below 1,200 base ATK: ATK% > CRIT Rate > CRIT DMG
1,200-1,600 base ATK: CRIT Rate = ATK% > CRIT DMG
Above 1,600 base ATK: CRIT Rate > CRIT DMG > ATK%
These are rough guidelines, not gospel. Test your own account.

Myth #5: "Skill Is More Important Than Gear"

I saved this one for last because I think it is the most controversial and the most misunderstood. The community loves the "skill over gear" narrative. It makes us feel good. It democratizes the game and suggests that anyone can clear content if they are good enough, regardless of their account quality. I have said this to other players myself. I believed it. And I have come to think it is mostly wrong.

Skill matters at the margins. A skilled player can squeeze 10-15% more performance out of the same account as an unskilled player through perfect dodging, optimal rotations, and good positioning. I have tested this. I recorded my floor 10 clear times over 20 attempts, then watched runs from a friend who is mechanically better than me but has a worse account. I cleared faster every single time, even though she made fewer mistakes, because my gear was simply better. The gear advantage was approximately 25%. Her skill advantage was approximately 10%. The gear won.

This is not a popular thing to say, because it implies that the game is pay-to-win to some degree, and nobody wants to admit that about a game they love. But here is the truth: Love and Deepspace is a gacha game with gear gating. It is designed so that gear is the primary gating mechanism and skill is the secondary one. You can be the most mechanically perfect player in the world, but if your memories are level 60 and your protocores are +6, you are not clearing floor 12. The game does not allow it. The HP pools are tuned assuming a certain power level, and skill cannot overcome a 40% power deficit.

The inverse is also true: you can play terribly and still clear most content if your gear is good enough. I know this because I have done it. There are nights when I am tired and I play sloppily, eating attacks I should be dodging, missing rotation windows, fumbling my energy management. I still clear. Not as quickly, not as cleanly, but the clear happens because my numbers are high enough to absorb my mistakes.

I am not saying skill does not matter. It matters a lot when two players have comparable gear. It matters when you are pushing content at the edge of your power level. It matters for speedruns and rankings. But for the average player stuck on a floor, the answer is usually gear, not skill. The community's reluctance to say this means that players who are hitting walls spend weeks trying to "get good" when they should be spending that time farming better protocores.

If you are stuck on a floor, ask yourself honestly: am I dying to mechanics I do not understand, or am I just not doing enough damage? The answer is usually the second one.

The practical implication: when you hit a wall, spend your first three attempts focusing entirely on mechanics. If you can survive the fight but run out of time, the answer is gear. If you are dying to specific attacks, the answer is practice. Do not mix the two. Gear problems require gear solutions. Skill problems require skill solutions. Throwing better gear at a skill problem will eventually work through brute force, but it is inefficient. Throwing practice at a gear problem will never work. Know which one you are dealing with.

Conclusion: Why I Am Writing This

I do not expect everyone to agree with these takes. In fact, I hope some of you disagree, because disagreement means engagement, and engagement means the community is thinking critically rather than repeating received wisdom. That is the goal of this article, really. Not to establish a new orthodoxy, but to challenge the existing one.

The Love and Deepspace community is one of the most helpful gaming communities I have been part of. People genuinely want to help each other. But good intentions do not always produce good advice. Myths persist because they sound correct, or because they were correct at one point and became outdated, or because they serve an emotional need (like the "skill over gear" narrative that makes us feel in control).

My advice: question everything. Test everything on your own account. Build a spreadsheet. Run your own numbers. The advice that got your friend to floor 12 might not be the advice that gets you there. Your account is different. Your playstyle is different. Your luck is different. The only person who can figure out the optimal path for your account is you.

Or just ignore all of this and play the game however you want. That is also valid. Probably more valid, honestly. The meta slaves are having fun in their own way, optimizing every substat and spreadsheet. But they are not having more fun than the person who just logs in, plays their favorite characters, and logs out. Fun is the only metric that actually matters.
