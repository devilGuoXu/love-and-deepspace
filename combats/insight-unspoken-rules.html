The Unspoken Rules of Love and Deepspace

By Kaelen — July 10, 2026 — 22 min read

I have spent 314 consecutive days logged into this game across two accounts. I have pulled on seventeen banners, whaled on three of them, and gone completely f2p on the second account just to see what it felt like. I have memorized the attack patterns of every boss to the point where I can dodge them with my eyes closed, which I tested once and do not recommend. This article is not a guide. There are plenty of those already, written by people who are better at math than me and who probably have healthier relationships with their stamina bars. This is a collection of things nobody told me, that I had to learn the hard way, so you do not have to.

1. Stamina Anxiety Is Real and It Never Gets Better

Everybody talks about the early game stamina crunch. You have a billion things to level, your energy bar empties in three battles, and you stare at the 12-hour refill timer like it personally insulted your family. What nobody talks about is that this feeling does not go away. It changes shape, but it does not go away.

I am sitting here at level 72 with a main team that can clear floor 12 of the Abyss, and I still open the game, look at my 140 stamina, and feel a wave of low-grade panic. What should I farm today? The protocore domain because mine are mediocre? The ascension domain because I am pre-farming for a memory I do not even own yet? The weekly boss because Tuesday is the most efficient day for it? I have spreadsheets. Actual Excel spreadsheets, with color coding and conditional formatting and a tab labeled "Stamina Projections" that I update every Monday. I am not proud of this. I am telling you this so you know what you are getting into.

The worst part is that even when you make the optimal choice, you immediately second-guess it. I farmed protocores for three days straight, got nothing usable, and spent the next week wondering if I should have farmed XP bottles instead. I farmed XP bottles, leveled a memory I never use, and spent the next three days wondering if I wasted two weeks of stamina. There is no winning. There is only the endless, grinding calculation of which loss is smallest.

Stamina efficiency is a myth the high-level players tell themselves to feel control over a system designed to forever make you want more. — Me, after my third cup of coffee at 2 AM doing protocore math

Here is the unspoken rule: pick two domains per week and commit. Do not check the daily rotation and recalculate. Do not ask Reddit what you should farm. Just pick. Monday-Tuesday you do protocores. Wednesday-Thursday you do ascension materials. Friday through Sunday you do whatever you want, which for me means more protocores because I have a problem. The best stamina strategy is not the mathematically optimal one. It is the one you will actually stick to without driving yourself crazy.

2. Your First SSR Pity Is Almost Always a Trap

I remember my first SSR pull vividly. It was on the standard banner, somewhere around pull 62. The golden light, the dramatic reveal animation, the rush of dopamine. I got Xavier's "Lightseeking" memory, which at the time I thought was incredible because the artwork was beautiful and Xavier was my favorite. I leveled it to 80 immediately. I spent all my resources on it. I built my entire early-game strategy around it.

Here is what I did not realize at the time: that memory was from the standard banner, and I could have gotten it eventually from the shard shop. I blew my new-account gems on standard banner pulls because I did not know that limited banners existed, or that pity carried over, or the difference between a rate-up and a standard pool SSR. I was not alone. Every single person I have talked to who started playing at launch made this exact mistake. The game does not explain banner types well. The standard banner SSR you get at soft pity feels like a gift from the universe. It is not a gift. It is a trap designed to make you think standard banner pulls are valuable.

They are not. Save every gem for limited banners. The standard banner will fill itself over time through free pulls, monthly passes, and battle passes. I am currently at 280 standard banner pulls completely for free, and I have gotten three SSRs from it. My second account, which I started with the explicit rule of "never spend gems on standard banner," has two standard SSRs at 190 pulls, which is actually better luck. The game gives you enough free standard pulls. Do not supplement them with gems.

But here is the thing I actually want to tell you: that first SSR, even if it was suboptimal, even if you regret it later, was probably exactly what you needed at the time. The game is designed so that your first SSR carries you through the early and mid-game regardless of what it is. I see new players agonizing over which standard banner memory to pick from the SSR box, reading tier lists, asking Reddit, paralyzed by choice. Pick the one you like the most. Seriously. The difference between S-tier and B-tier on the standard banner is maybe 10% performance. Having a character you enjoy using is worth way more than 10%.

3. The Mid-Game Slump Is Where Accounts Go to Die

There is a specific point in every Love and Deepspace account's lifecycle that I call the Valley of Despair. It hits somewhere between level 45 and 55, usually around the time you finish the main story and realize that all the guaranteed gem sources are gone. The world map is fully explored. The achievements are starting to get expensive in terms of effort per gem. Your team is good enough to clear most content but not good enough to handle Abyss floors 7 and above. You look at your resource stockpile and realize it would take three weeks of farming to level one single memory to 80.

This is where most alt accounts die. This is where people quit. I know because my first alt account is rotting at level 48 with 40 unread messages and a half-leveled team that I abandoned in a fit of frustration after failing Abyss floor 6 for the fifth time. I came back to it three months later, pushed through the wall, and now it is clearing floor 10. The resources were there. I just could not see them from the bottom of the valley.

The unspoken rule of the mid-game slump is that it is not about resources. It is about expectations. You spent the early game getting showered with gems and materials and new memories, and you got used to the feeling of constant progress. Then the shower turns off, and you are left with the slow trickle of daily login rewards, and it feels like you have stopped progressing entirely. But you have not. Your account is still growing. The growth is just happening in smaller increments that are harder to feel.

The mid-game does not ask whether you have enough resources. It asks whether you have enough patience.

My advice for the valley: stop checking your gem count. Stop calculating how many more days until pity. Stop comparing yourself to launch players who have been farming for a year. Set one goal per week, not per day. This week, I will get my main DPS to level 70. Next week, I will get their protocores to +12. The week after, I will attempt Abyss floor 7 once per day just to practice the patterns. Small goals. Measurable progress. The valley has an exit, but you have to walk through it, not sprint.

4. Protocore Farming Is the Real Endgame, and It Is Miserable

I want to talk about protocores, because I think the game design around them is genuinely fascinating and also genuinely evil. Protocores are the most important gear system in the game. They provide stats that determine whether you clear content or hit a wall. They are also completely random, expensive to enhance, and offer zero pity protection on substat rolls. You can farm for a month and get nothing useful. You can get a perfect piece on your first run and never see another one for six months.

I have tracked my protocore drops over 500 domain runs on my main account. Here are the numbers, pulled straight from my notes app where I obsessively logged every run for two months before realizing I had a problem:

Three perfect pieces in 500 runs. That is a 0.6% rate per run, or one perfect piece every 167 runs. A perfect piece means the main stat is correct AND at least three of the four substats are useful AND the substat rolls go into the right stats. I am including pieces where two of the four rolls went into the correct stat as "usable" and pieces where three or more went into correct stats as "perfect."

The community math says you need approximately 12 usable protocores for a full team. That is 12 usable pieces at approximately 28 runs per usable piece, meaning 336 runs for a full team's worth of protocores. At 40 stamina per run, that is 13,440 stamina, or 56 days of natural stamina regen if you spend every single point on protocores. That is almost two months of doing nothing but protocore farming for a single team. And then the meta changes and a new set comes out and you start over.

I am not telling you this to scare you. I am telling you this so you understand that when you see a whale with perfect substats on every piece, you are looking at either someone who has been farming since launch or someone who bought stamina packs. Probably both. The unspoken rule: protocores are a marathon, not a sprint. Do not burn yourself out trying to get perfect gear in a week. Settle for "good enough" on your first three pieces and chase perfection on the last two. The difference between "good" protocores and "perfect" protocores is maybe 8% damage. The difference between "bad" protocores and "good" protocores is 40%. Focus on getting everything to "good" before you chase "perfect."

5. You Will Eventually Main Someone You Did Not Expect

I started this game as a Xavier main. I picked him on day one, read all his messages first, leveled his memories highest, convinced myself he was my absolute favorite. I had Xavier wallpapers on my phone. I spent money on Xavier banners. I was a Xavier Main with capital letters and a Reddit flair.

Around month four, I accidentally pulled Sylus's "Midnight Whisper" memory from a limited banner. I did not even want it. I was pulling for the 4-star that was rate-up and got the SSR at hard pity. I was irritated. I threw the memory on a team slot because I had leveled it for the stat stick and did not want to waste the resources.

By month six, Sylus was my most-used operative. By month eight, I had pulled for his next banner intentionally. By month ten, I had stopped pretending and updated my flair.

This happened to everyone I know. My friend who swore she was a Zayne-only account accidentally built Rafayel because she got his BiS memory from a free pull and now Rafayel is her second most-used. My other friend who was staunchly "no husbandos, only meta" ended up maining Xavier because she liked his combat animation the most. The game has a way of pushing you toward the operative that actually fits your playstyle, regardless of who you thought you liked at the start.

The unspoken rule: do not force yourself to main someone because of their personality if their combat style does not click with you. You can enjoy a character's story without maining them in combat. I still read all of Xavier's new content. I still love his character. I just do not use him for floor 12 anymore, and that is okay. The game is more fun when you play who works, not who you feel obligated to play.

6. The Community Is Simultaneously the Best and Worst Part

I have mixed feelings about the Love and Deepspace community, and I think it is healthy to be honest about this. On one hand, the theorycrafting community on Discord and Reddit has produced genuinely incredible work. The damage formula derivation, the substat probability models, the stamina efficiency calculators. People have put hundreds of hours into understanding this game at a mechanical level, and they share that work for free. I have used community resources to save months of trial and error. I am grateful for that.

On the other hand, the community has a toxicity problem around character preference that I have never seen in any other gacha game. People get genuinely angry about which fictional character you like more. There are shipping wars that spill into gameplay discussions. There are players who refuse to build certain operatives because they do not like the character, and then complain that the content is too hard. There are players who build every operative equally and then complain that they cannot clear any content well. The tribalism is exhausting.

My least favorite community trend is the purity testing around spending. F2P players who act morally superior to spenders. Whales who act like F2P players are freeloaders. The truth is that the game needs both to exist. F2P players provide the playerbase that makes matchmaking work and keeps the community active. Whales provide the revenue that keeps the servers running. Neither is better. Neither is worse. If you spend money, good for you. If you do not, also good for you. The only wrong way to engage with the game's monetization is to spend more than you can afford, and that is between you and your bank account, not between you and Reddit.

I have learned to curate my community experience aggressively. I follow theorycrafters and ignore the drama. I participate in the fan art threads and skip the ship wars. I ask for build advice in the help channels and mute the general discussion. You have to build your own experience. The unspoken rule: take what is useful from the community and leave the rest. You do not have to engage with every argument. You are allowed to just play the game.

7. The Guides Are All Lying to You about "Efficiency"

I have read approximately forty thousand stamina efficiency guides. Some of them are genuinely useful. Most of them are trying to optimize for a scenario that does not exist. They assume you have infinite time, perfect information, and the discipline of a Buddhist monk. They tell you to never farm anything that is not at maximum domain level. They tell you to always wait for double-drop events. They tell you to never enhance a protocore that does not have at least three usable substats.

Here is the thing: if you follow all of that advice, you will spend most of your playtime doing nothing. You will be waiting. Waiting for the right domain to be open. Waiting for the double-drop event. Waiting for the perfect protocore to drop. You will log in, do your dailies, look at your full stamina bar, and close the game because none of the available options are "efficient" enough. And then your stamina bar will sit at full for eight hours while you are at work, wasting all the efficiency you were trying to preserve.

I have a theory: stamina efficiency guides are written by people who have already finished farming everything, looking back at their journey and reverse-engineering optimal paths. But they are writing for an audience that is still in the middle of the journey, and the paths that were optimal for them are not optimal for someone who is, say, stuck on Abyss floor 5 right now and needs a 10% power increase by tomorrow, not a 30% power increase in three months.

The optimal long-term strategy is worthless if it makes you quit the game in the short term.

The unspoken rule: efficiency is a goal, not a commandment. If you need to spend 60 stamina on a domain two levels below your maximum because it is the only one open and you have fifteen minutes to play before work, do it. The amount of efficiency you lose is tiny compared to the efficiency you lose from letting stamina sit capped. The absolute worst thing you can do for your account's progress is not farming suboptimally. It is not farming at all.

8. The Emotional Cost of Pulling for Your Favorite

I want to talk about something that no guide covers, because it is not mechanical and it is not financial. It is emotional. Pulling for a limited character in this game is genuinely stressful. You save for weeks, sometimes months. You watch your gem count go up, and you feel good. Then the banner drops, and you start pulling, and your gem count goes down, and you feel a type of anxiety that I have only ever felt in casinos, which I stopped going to because of that exact feeling.

The first time I whaled, it was for Sylus's second limited banner. I had saved 120 pulls. I was feeling confident. Then I lost the 50-50 at pull 75. I got a standard SSR that I already had. I stared at the screen for probably thirty seconds. Then I opened my wallet, which I had told myself I would not do. I bought the biggest gem pack. I pulled another 75. I got Sylus. I felt relief, not joy. I had spent real money, and what I felt was the absence of dread, not the presence of happiness.

I have thought about that moment a lot since then. The game is designed to create that feeling. The 50-50 is not a bonus mechanic. It is a psychological mechanism that makes you value what you won with luck less than what you won after losing, because the loss creates an emotional investment that the win resolves. It is the same psychological pattern that keeps people playing slot machines, and I do not say that to judge anyone. I say it because I think it is important to recognize it.

I still spend money on this game. I have accepted that. I budget for it like I budget for eating out or going to the movies. But I no longer pull when I am upset, or tired, or drunk. I no longer pull on the last day of the banner because of FOMO. I set a budget before the banner starts, and when it is gone, I stop, regardless of whether I got what I wanted. That is the unspoken rule: know your limit before you start pulling, not after. The game will not tell you when to stop. You have to decide that for yourself.

9. Boss Patterns You Only Notice after the Fiftieth Kill

There is a category of game knowledge that I think of as "deep pattern recognition." It is the stuff you cannot learn from a guide because a guide only has room to describe what is visible and measurable. The real mastery of boss fights comes from the sensory details that you pick up only after fighting the same boss dozens of times.

For example: the Crystal Monarch has a specific breathing animation that changes speed about one second before she summons icicles. It is subtle. Her chest rises and falls at a steady rhythm for most of the fight, and then, right before the icicle attack, the rhythm changes to a faster, shallower pattern. I have watched videos of this boss to confirm I was not imagining it, and I was not. It is there. It is consistent. And I only noticed it on maybe my fortieth kill, when I was not focusing on anything in particular and just watching the boss out of boredom.

The Inferno Colossus has a sound cue that most players miss. When he is about to do the lava pool attack, there is a low rumble that starts about half a second before the visual telegraph. It is at the very bottom of the audio mix, almost below the music. If you play with headphones and turn the music volume down slightly, you can hear it. It gives you an extra half-second of reaction time, which is the difference between eating the lava pool and dodging it cleanly.

The Phantom Sylus fight has a tell that I have never seen anyone mention online. When he is about to do his teleport strike, he looks at you. Not in the "this enemy is tracking the player" way that most enemies do. He specifically turns his head to face the camera, holds eye contact for about 0.3 seconds, and then teleports. It is a deliberate animation choice that signals his aggression. If you watch his head, not his body, you will never get hit by the teleport again.

These are the things that separate a good player from a great player in this game. Not reaction time. Not gear. Not even game knowledge in the traditional sense. It is the willingness to pay attention to the small things, to learn the boss's language beyond what the attack tells tell you. The unspoken rule: if you are stuck on a boss, stop trying to do more damage. Spend five runs doing nothing but watching the boss. Do not attack. Just dodge and watch. You will notice things you never saw when you were focused on your rotation.

10. Abyss Floor 5 Is the Real Skill Check, Not Floor 12

I want to push back on a community consensus. Everyone talks about Abyss floor 12 as the ultimate test of an account. They post their floor 12 clears, discuss floor 12 strategies, agonize over floor 12 gear checks. And sure, floor 12 is hard. But floor 5 is the real bottleneck. Floor 5 is where the game stops holding your hand and starts asking real questions about your account's fundamentals.

Floor 5 is the first floor that requires two teams. It is the first floor where Stellactrum matching becomes mandatory instead of nice-to-have. It is the first floor where the enemy levels outpace your account level in a meaningful way. I have seen accounts with stacked gear on their main team fail floor 5 because their second team had no matching Stellactrum and got one-shot by the elemental activation. I have seen accounts with mediocre gear clear floor 5 easily because they had two balanced teams instead of one super-team and one sacrificial lamb.

The lesson of floor 5 is that account breadth matters more than account depth at certain thresholds. You need four operatives leveled, not two. You need two sets of protocores, not one. You cannot borrow your main DPS's protocores for the second team because both teams fight simultaneously. Floor 5 exposes accounts that are "tall but narrow," and the fix is almost never about getting better gear. It is about spreading your resources more evenly across a second team.

I spent two weeks stuck on floor 5 because I refused to level a second team. When I finally did, I cleared it on the first try. The wall was not gear. It was stubbornness. — A message I sent to a friend that I think about a lot

The unspoken rule: before you invest in making your main team stronger for floor 12, make sure your second team can survive floor 5. Floor 12 rewards are better, but floor 5 rewards are guaranteed if you have the roster depth. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush, and all that.

11. You Are Playing More Optimally Than You Think

I see a lot of anxiety in the community about whether people are playing "correctly." Am I farming the right domains? Am I leveling the right memories? Am I using the right team composition? The amount of analysis paralysis in this community is genuinely concerning. People spend more time worrying about whether they are playing efficiently than actually playing the game.

Here is a truth that I have arrived at after nearly a year of playing: unless you are doing something obviously wrong like leveling every memory to 20 or feeding SSR protocores into SR ones, you are probably playing within 10-15% of optimal. The difference between a good build and a perfect build is small. The difference between a well-played fight and a perfectly-played fight is small. The difference between your current progress and your theoretical maximum progress is mostly time, not decisions.

The people who are clearing floor 12 did not make better decisions than you. They just started earlier, or spent more, or got luckier on their protocore rolls. And that last one is the one nobody wants to talk about. Luck is a massive factor in this game. Two players can make identical decisions for six months and end up with completely different accounts because one of them got three perfect protocore pieces and the other got zero. That is not a skill difference. That is variance.

The unspoken rule: give yourself credit for what you have accomplished. The game is designed to make you feel like you are always behind, because that feeling drives engagement. But you are probably doing fine. If you are clearing the content that is available to you and having fun while doing it, you are playing correctly. There is no wrong way to play a game that you are enjoying.

12. What Happens When You Finally Beat the Game

I reached a point around month nine where I had cleared every piece of content in the game. All Abyss floors at 3 stars. All event content completed. All story content finished. All achievements that did not require specific gacha memories. I had, by any reasonable standard, beaten the game. I sat on the main menu for about five minutes, looking at my account, waiting to feel something. Completion. Satisfaction. Release. I did not feel any of those things.

I felt empty. Not in a dramatic, existential way. More like I had been climbing a mountain and suddenly realized there was no summit, just another plateau with another mountain behind it. The content I was clearing was going to reset. The next banner was going to drop. The next power level was going to be introduced. The game does not end. It just keeps going, forever, asking you to do the same things slightly harder each time.

I am not saying this to be depressing. I am saying it because I think the expectation that you will eventually "finish" the game is a trap. You will not finish it. The game is designed to never be finished. The moment you catch up to the content curve, the game moves the curve. The only way to truly finish is to stop caring about being caught up, which is easier said than done when the game is designed to make you care.

What I have found is that the game is most enjoyable when I treat it as a background activity rather than a main quest. I do my dailies. I make incremental progress. I look forward to new story updates. I engage with the content I enjoy and skip the content I do not. When I stopped trying to beat the game and started just living in it, the experience got better. The unspoken rule: the game is a journey, not a destination. Clichés exist because they are true. You will never log in one day and see a "Congratulations, you win" screen. You will just keep playing until you do not want to anymore, and that is okay too.

13. You Are Having More Fun Than You Think

I want to end this on a positive note, because despite everything I have said, despite the stamina anxiety and the protocore misery and the emotional cost of whaling, I genuinely love this game. I have played it for 314 consecutive days. I think about it when I am not playing it. I have written tens of thousands of words about it across multiple articles. I have made friends through the community. I have had moments of genuine joy pulling a character I wanted, clearing a floor I was stuck on, discovering a detail in the story that I had missed.

The problem with games-as-a-service is that they condition us to focus on what we do not have rather than what we do. The game is always showing you the next thing you could unlock, the next character you could pull, the next power spike you could achieve. It is easy to look at your account and see only the gaps. The memories you are missing. The gear that is not perfect. The floors you have not cleared.

But look at what you have. Look at the team you built, the progress you made, the story you experienced. Look at the character you love, the one who makes you smile when they appear on screen. That is the real game. That is what you are actually playing for, even if the stamina efficiency guides tell you otherwise.

The unspoken rule of Love and Deepspace, the one that I had to learn the hard way over 314 days and two accounts and more protocore runs than I care to count: you are allowed to enjoy the game imperfectly. You are allowed to make suboptimal decisions. You are allowed to pull for a character because you like their design, not because they are meta. You are allowed to take a break. You are allowed to care too much, and you are allowed to not care at all. The game will be here when you come back, waiting for you, with the same stamina bar and the same banners and the same endless, beautiful, frustrating grind.

And that is not a bad thing. It is just the game. Play it your way.
